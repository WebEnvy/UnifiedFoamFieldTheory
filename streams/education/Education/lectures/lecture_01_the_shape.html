Step 1

Meet the truncated octahedron

This is the shape that might explain everything. 24 vertices, 36 edges, 14 faces. It has two kinds of faces — 8 hexagons and 6 squares.

Rotate it. Look at it from every angle. It's the simplest solid that fills all of 3D space with no gaps and no overlaps.

Drag to rotate · Scroll to zoom · Click faces to highlight them
Step 2

Two kinds of faces

The 8 hexagonal faces sit at the corners — one in each direction where you'd point if you were inside the shape pointing diagonally. The 6 square faces sit at the flat sides — top, bottom, front, back, left, right.

This two-face structure turns out to matter enormously. In the language of the framework: hexagons carry bubbles, squares carry voids.

Click "Show hex" and "Show square" to highlight each type
Step 3

Space-filling

Lord Kelvin asked in 1887: what's the most efficient way to divide space into equal cells? His answer was this shape — the truncated octahedron. Pack them together and every face is shared with a neighbour. No gaps, no waste.

Imagine the entire universe filled with these, at the Planck scale — 10⁻³⁵ metres across. That's the foam.

Click "Tile foam" to see cells pack together
Step 4

Why this shape?

Five shapes fill space. Only one — this one — has the mathematical structure to produce the particles and forces we observe. Its face vibrations produce exactly the right eigenvalues, with a prime discriminant (17), and the right number of faces (14) for three-generation physics.

That's where we're going. But first, you needed to meet the shape.
